The Rosettes

Provenance
Possibly acquired by Rudolf II of Bohemia, who is said to have paid 600 ducats for it; then the botanist Jacobus Horčický de Tepenecz, whose erased signature survives on f1r; then Georg Baresch of Prague, who sent it to Athanasius Kircher in Rome by 1666; bought by Wilfrid Voynich from the Jesuit library at Villa Mondragone, Frascati, in 1912, and given to Yale in 1969.

The Rosettes — nine medallions joined by causeways and castle-like forms, the largest spread in the manuscript. A six-panel foldout that opens far beyond a single leaf.

Cipher reading

Under the cipher reading, the central rosette's label is the key to the whole foldout — a place-name or cosmological term whose decryption would unlock the map's geography. Hoax reading

Under the grille reading, the label is decorative gibberish; the causeways and castles are an impressive visual forgery, and the text laid over them carries no place-names because it carries nothing.

View on the image
Natural-language reading

Under the abjad reading, the label names a real place or region; the abjad hypothesis is what motivates reading the Rosettes as an actual (if stylised) geographic or cosmographic diagram.

View on the image

**The Rosettes foldout, as a whole object.** The nine medallions, their joining causeways, and the six-panel fold structure are properties of the spread *entire* (f85v–86r) — the largest object in the manuscript. Attached to the whole object (no box), so the Viewer frames the foldout rather than pinning one rosette.

View on the image

**The north-west rosette.** One medallion of the nine, traced round its rim rather than boxed: the causeways that leave it at four and eight o'clock belong to the joins, not to this circle, and a rectangle drawn here would claim them. The manuscript's own geometry is round, so the annotation is too.
